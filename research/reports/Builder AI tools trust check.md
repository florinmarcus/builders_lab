# Builders carry the risk AI tools disclaim

No AI tool sold to UK builders publishes how often its answers are wrong, and every tool whose terms we could read puts the legal duty for its output back on the builder. That is the main finding of a check of about 35 tools on 19 Nov 2026: small AI-first tools (regulation chatbots, RAMS generators, quoting apps), trades and accounting apps, big construction platforms, and general assistants like ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot and Gemini. Trust evidence follows company size. Xero, Procore, Autodesk, Microsoft, Google and Anthropic publish audited security certificates and clear statements on AI training. Most small tools publish none, and several do not name the company behind them. But size is not the whole story: a few small UK tools (Ramsgen, RAMSReady) publish clearer data terms than some well-known trades apps (Tradify, Jobber). The biggest everyday risk is not a niche tool at all. It is a builder pasting a client's name, address or a council tender into a free ChatGPT or Claude account, where chats can be used for training and there is no data processing agreement. This matters because surveys report that most builders now use AI, mostly for admin and quotes, which is exactly where client data goes in. All findings come from vendor pages read on 19 Nov 2026. We did not test the products, and we did not check any certificate against a register, so every certificate below is "claimed", not "verified". "Not stated" means we looked and did not find it; it does not mean the vendor does not do it.

## Not one tool shows how often its AI gets things wrong

Across all four groups, **no vendor publishes an accuracy rate, a test set or an error rate** for its AI. The regulation chatbots come closest to good practice by showing sources. StructureGPT gives "source document, section, and page" for every answer, says plainly that it covers England only, and warns that it "does not constitute professional... advice" ([StructureGPT](https://structuregpt.co.uk)). Struck and BuildingRegs.app also cite clauses ([Struck](https://struck.eu/uk); [BuildingRegs.app](https://buildingregs.app)). A citation helps a builder check an answer, but it does not prove the answer is right, and no tool reports how often its citations actually support what it says.

Some marketing claims go much further than any published evidence. Struck says it uses verified databases "to ensure **0% hallucination on legal facts**", but gives no test behind the figure, and its UK library lists some older editions of Approved Documents ([Struck](https://struck.eu/uk)). BuildwellAI's About page promises "Assured Compliance" and "fully compliant designs", while its own terms say it "does not warrant that any of the materials on its website are accurate, complete or current" ([BuildwellAI about](https://buildwellai.com/about); [BuildwellAI terms](https://buildwellai.com/terms)). BuildwellAI's main product is also still behind a waitlist ([BuildwellAI](https://buildwellai.com)). A Designing Buildings article reports that AI platforms it looked at were not trained on UK Building Regulations and sometimes produced fabricated references ([Designing Buildings](https://www.designingbuildings.co.uk/wiki/AI-Driven_automation_in_architectural_design:_Reducing_time,_enhancing_compliance)); we read only a summary of that article.

The better tools limit what the AI can make up, rather than claiming it never errs. **RAMS AI keeps the AI away from the safety content**: hazards and controls come from a library "reviewed by a qualified health and safety consultant", and "the AI only tidies the wording" ([RAMS AI](https://ramsai.co.uk)). Ramsgen says "If it isn't cited, it's not used", grounding its output in 52 HSE publications and approved codes of practice ([Ramsgen](https://www.ramsgen.com)). Neither names the reviewer or publishes test results, so the design is sound but unproven. In trades and accounting apps the common good pattern is **human approval before any action**: ServiceM8's Office Agent "prepares supported actions for your approval", Fergus shows a confirmation step before anything is created or sent, and TradGo says estimates "always go through your approval" ([ServiceM8](https://support.servicem8.com/help-center/servicem8-add-ons/office-agent/introducing-office-agent); [Fergus](https://fergus.com/llm-info/); [TradGo](https://tradgo-site.pages.dev/)). Intuit is unusually frank: its UK launch says "Intuit Intelligence can make mistakes", and it uses "deterministic code where precision matters" ([Intuit UK](https://quickbooks.intuit.com/uk/press/intuit-intelligence-now-available-internationally/)).

Among the big platforms, **Autodesk is the only one that publishes per-feature AI Transparency Cards**, covering which models sit behind a feature, what data it uses, its limits and its safeguards ([Autodesk News](https://adsknews.autodesk.com/en/?post_type=news&p=59885)). AEC Magazine points out that the cards need dates and version histories, because models change ([AEC Magazine](https://aecmag.com/ai/autodesk-puts-updated-ai-transparency-cards-inside-assistant/)). Procore lists an "AI Accuracy and Transparency" document on its trust centre, but the content is behind an access request ([Procore Trust Center](https://trust.procore.com/)). One small tool works against independent testing: **DocGen's terms forbid users to "publish or share performance benchmarks or comparisons"** ([DocGen terms](https://app.docgen.co.uk/terms)).

## Security evidence follows company size, with a few small exceptions

The large vendors publish audited evidence. **Xero states ISO/IEC 27001:2022 certification and produces SOC 2 reports**, available after login ([Xero security](https://www.xero.com/uk/security/)). **Procore lists ISO 27001:2022, SOC 1 and SOC 2 Type 2** and hosts UK users' application data in a UK zone ([Procore Trust Center](https://trust.procore.com/); [Procore infrastructure](https://www.procore.com/en-gb/trust-and-security/infrastructure)). Google says Gemini in Workspace holds ISO 42001, the AI management standard, plus SOC reports ([Google Workspace AI privacy](https://workspace.google.com/security/ai-privacy/)). Anthropic announced an accredited ISO 42001 certificate in January 2025 ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-achieves-iso-42001-certification-for-responsible-ai)), and Autodesk says its AI principles are validated through ISO 42001 ([Autodesk](https://adsknews.autodesk.com/en/stories/how-autodesk-builds-trusted-ai/)). ISO 42001 certifies how a company manages AI, not whether any single model is accurate ([aicompliancevendors.com](https://aicompliancevendors.com/blog/iso-42001-certified-companies-list)).

Buildertrend and Houzz Pro mostly quote their cloud host's certificates. Houzz Pro lists AWS's SOC 2 and ISO 27001, not its own ([Houzz Pro FAQ](https://www.houzz.com/pro-help/r/houzz-pro-data-security--privacy-faq)), and Buildertrend's FAQ points to its hosting facilities' SOC 2 report ([Buildertrend FAQ](https://buildertrend.com/frequently-asked-questions/)). "Our host is certified" is real but narrower than "we were audited".

Among trades apps, **Joblogic announces Cyber Essentials Plus** ([Joblogic](https://www.joblogic.com/blog/joblogic-achieves-cyber-essentials-plus-accreditation/)), which matters because council buyers often treat Cyber Essentials as a baseline. Commusoft gives the most technical detail: Google Cloud hosting including the UK, encryption at rest, and a yearly external penetration test, but its live page does not mention ISO 27001 ([Commusoft security](https://www.commusoft.com/security)). Tradify's security page returns a 404, and we found no certificate published by Tradify, Fergus, ServiceM8, Jobber or YourTradebase ([Tradify](https://www.tradifyhq.com/security)). Among the small AI tools, only two claim a certificate: **Ramsgen says "Cyber Essentials Plus certified"** and **Struck says ISO 27001**, with no certificate number or certifier shown ([Ramsgen](https://www.ramsgen.com); [Struck](https://struck.eu/uk)). Both claims can be checked: Cyber Essentials on the IASME register, ISO 27001 with the certifying body. We did not do that check.

Who is behind a tool is also part of security. Of the small tools, **four of the seven companies we could trace were set up in the last 15 months**. Sleepless Tradesman Ltd was incorporated on 23 Oct 2026, less than a month before our check, and stores job and HMRC data with US hosting ([Companies House](https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk/company/17538029); [Sleepless Tradesman privacy](https://sleeplesstradesman.com/privacy)). For **DocGen, RAMS PRO, TradeQuoteAI and QuoteSmith we found no company name or number**, and QuickPrice gives a Bristol address but no number ([QuickPrice privacy](https://getquickprice.com/privacy); [Companies House search](https://find-and-update.company-information.service.gov.uk/search/companies?q=QuickPrice)). Two AI receptionists, TradGo and TradesCall AI, run on free `pages.dev` web addresses with no security or data location stated ([TradGo](https://tradgo-site.pages.dev/); [TradesCall AI](https://tradescall-ai.pages.dev/)). Being new or small is not proof of bad practice. It does mean there is little to check, and less certainty the tool will exist next year.

## Data protection splits on the plan you pay for and on the exact words

For general assistants, **the plan matters more than the brand**. ChatGPT Free, Plus and Pro use chats for training by default unless the user opts out, and have no data processing agreement (DPA); ChatGPT Business and Enterprise exclude business data from training by default and come with a DPA ([Janus Compliance](https://www.januscompliance.co.uk/blog/is-chatgpt-gdpr-compliant-plan-by-plan-2026)). Since September 2025, **Claude's consumer plans let users choose whether chats train models, and keep them for five years if they agree**; Team, Enterprise and API use fall under commercial terms with no training by default ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/updates-to-our-consumer-terms)). Google says it does not use Workspace data to train the models behind Gemini outside Workspace without permission ([Google Workspace](https://workspace.google.com/security/ai-privacy/)). The NCSC's advice since 2023 is simple: do not put sensitive information in queries to public AI tools, because the provider can see and store them and may change its policy later ([NCSC](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blog-post/chatgpt-and-large-language-models-whats-the-risk)).

"Stays in the UK" is harder to get than many builders assume. Microsoft says Copilot queries from customers outside the EU may be processed in the US, the EU or other regions ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/copilot/microsoft-365-copilot-privacy)). Secondary sources disagree on when UK processing arrives, and report that Anthropic's Claude models in Copilot may be switched on by default for UK tenants from 9 December unless an admin sets the control ([Beri.net](https://www.beri.net/article/microsoft-365-copilot-claude-default-on-december-9-uk-eu-efta-eu-data-boundary-mc1546435-anthropic-subprocessor-opt-out); [TechsCurrent](https://techscurrent.com/2026/11/claude-microsoft-copilot-eu-data-boundary-admin-checklist/)). Microsoft 365 admins should check this setting before December.

The exact wording of "no training" claims matters. **Xero is the clearest**: JAX chat "doesn't use your data to train the LLMs it uses", and it names AWS, Azure, Google Cloud and OpenAI as providers ([Xero JAX](https://www.xero.com/uk/jax/)). Procore says data "is never used to train models like ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini" ([Procore AI](https://www.procore.com/ai)), and Houzz Pro says client data is "never used to train public AI tools" ([Houzz Pro FAQ](https://www.houzz.com/pro-help/r/houzz-pro-data-security--privacy-faq)). Both statements cover outside models; neither says whether the vendor trains its own models. Jobber's terms say AI chats "may be logged for analysis, improvement, and security purposes" and that it may use de-identified content to improve the service ([Jobber terms](https://getjobber.com/terms-of-service/)). Tradify added AI "SmartTools" but its privacy policy does not mention AI, UK GDPR or its sub-processors ([Tradify privacy](https://www.tradifyhq.com/privacy-policy)).

Some of the smallest tools do this best. **RAMSReady names every processor with its location** (Anthropic and Google for AI, Hetzner in Finland for data), says paid API terms stop the AI providers training on content, and publishes a DPA with a 24-hour breach notice, adding honestly that "they are not solicitor drafted" ([RAMSReady privacy](https://ramsready.co.uk/privacy); [RAMSReady DPA](https://ramsready.co.uk/dpa)). **Ramsgen holds all customer data at rest in Google Cloud London** and says its OpenAI account uses zero data retention, with a DPA and a sub-processor list ([Ramsgen privacy](https://www.ramsgen.com/privacy); [Ramsgen DPA](https://www.ramsgen.com/dpa)). QuoteSmith states UK hosting and names Anthropic ([QuoteSmith privacy](https://quotesmith.co.uk/privacy.html)). At the other end, BuildingRegs.app's privacy policy is one paragraph that names no AI provider, location or retention period ([BuildingRegs.app privacy](https://buildingregs.app/privacy)), and StructureGPT keeps queries for up to two years "for service improvement" without saying whether that includes training ([Atlasync privacy](https://www.atlasync.co.uk/privacy)).

AI receptionists add a specific gap: **call recording consent is left to the builder**. Jobber "recommends" telling callers that AI answers or the call is recorded, but says this is not required and points to "local or state regulations", which is US wording ([Jobber Receptionist](https://help.getjobber.com/hc/en-us/articles/25315927533847-Receptionist-powered-by-Jobber-AI)). Powered Now's receptionist records calls and does not state whether callers are told it is AI ([Powered Now](https://powerednow.com/receptionist/)). No receptionist vendor we checked states how long it keeps recordings.

## Homepages promise compliance, the terms hand it back

The legal pattern is the same almost everywhere: a confident claim on the homepage and a full disclaimer in the terms. RAMS PRO claims "full compliance with CDM 2015 regulations and ISO 45001 standards", but its terms page returned "404 - Not found" and no company is named ([RAMS PRO](https://ramspro.co.uk); [RAMS PRO terms](https://ramspro.co.uk/terms)). TradeQuoteAI shows a "GDPR Compliant" badge, which is self-declared: no certificate for GDPR compliance exists in the sense a badge suggests ([TradeQuoteAI](https://tradequoteai.com)). RAMSReady markets documents "Scored against the HSE standard" and "No safety qualification needed", yet its own FAQ says "no document is 'compliant' until the responsible person has reviewed it" and its footer says it "does not provide a guarantee of compliance" ([RAMSReady](https://ramsready.co.uk)). We know of no official HSE scoring standard for RAMS; our reading is that "HSE standard" means built from HSE guidance, not endorsed by HSE. To be fair, RAMSReady and Ramsgen both state the law correctly: under regulation 3 of the Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations 1999, the duty and sign-off stay with the employer's competent person ([Ramsgen](https://www.ramsgen.com)).

Liability is capped low. **Most small tools cap liability at about 12 months of fees**, sometimes with a floor of £100 or £1,000 ([RAMSReady terms](https://ramsready.co.uk/terms); [QuickPrice terms](https://getquickprice.com/terms); [DocGen terms](https://app.docgen.co.uk/terms); [RAMS AI terms](https://ramsai.co.uk/terms)). Jobber's terms say "you are solely responsible" for AI output, treat any AI agent action "as your action", cap liability at the greater of US$100 or fees paid, and are governed by Canadian law ([Jobber terms](https://getjobber.com/terms-of-service/)). Xero's JAX beta terms say not to rely on outputs "for accounting, financial or professional advice" ([Xero JAX beta terms](https://www.xero.com/us/legal/terms/just-ask-xero-beta-terms-of-use)). Buildertrend's agreement says generative AI output may be incorrect and puts the risk on the user ([Buildertrend](https://buildertrend.com/web-services-terms-and-conditions/)). For a builder, this means an AI-written quote, RAMS or client message is legally the builder's own.

Tax claims can be checked against a public list. **HMRC's Making Tax Digital for VAT finder lists QuickBooks, Xero and Powered Now**; Tradify, Fergus, ServiceM8, Jobber, YourTradebase, Joblogic and Commusoft are not on it ([HMRC MTD finder](https://www.tax.service.gov.uk/making-tax-digital-software)). Those apps rely on syncing to accounting software for MTD, and Tradify's reverse-charge VAT works only through Sage with CIS enabled ([Tradify help](https://help.tradifyhq.com/hc/en-us/articles/900005245206)). HMRC says listed software has passed its recognition process but that it "does not recommend any product" ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/find-software-thats-compatible-with-making-tax-digital-for-income-tax)). We could not reach the MTD for Income Tax list. On the EU AI Act, whose transparency rules have applied since 2 Aug 2026 ([Legalithm](https://www.legalithm.com/en/blog/eu-ai-act-what-applies-2-august-2026)), **none of the construction platforms or assistants we checked published a statement** of their position. That is a fair "not stated", and matters mainly to firms with EU clients.

## What a builder should check before pasting client or council data

Surveys show why this matters now. The FMB and CIOB State of Trade survey (Nov 2026) reports that 57% of construction businesses use AI more than a year ago, and among users 80% use it for admin and 42% for quotes, estimates and tenders ([Specification Online](https://specificationonline.co.uk/sections/sustainability/articles/2026-11-11/ciob/builders-embrace-ai-as-pressures-mount-across-construction)). RICS's 2026 report says privacy and security concern among respondents rose from 22% to 30% ([RICS 2026](https://www.rics.org/news-insights/ai-in-commercial-property-and-construction-report-2026)). UK GDPR applies in full when AI is used: the builder is the controller for client data, and the vendor is a processor only under a business plan with a DPA ([GDPR Local summary of ICO guidance](https://gdprlocal.com/ico-artificial-intelligence-navigating-ai-compliance-and-governance/)).

From the NCSC and ICO guidance and the vendor evidence above, a short set of checks covers most of the risk. The first two cost little and remove the biggest risk.

| # | Check | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Use a business plan (ChatGPT Business, Claude Team, Copilot with a work account, Gemini in Workspace), never a free or personal account, for client data | Free plans can train on chats and have no DPA ([Janus](https://www.januscompliance.co.uk/blog/is-chatgpt-gdpr-compliant-plan-by-plan-2026); [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/updates-to-our-consumer-terms)) |
| 2 | Ask for the DPA and a written "we do not train on your data", for any model, not just "public" ones | Wording varies ([Procore](https://www.procore.com/ai); [Houzz Pro](https://www.houzz.com/pro-help/r/houzz-pro-data-security--privacy-faq)) |
| 3 | Ask which AI company sees the data and where it is stored and processed | Many small tools do not say ([BuildingRegs.app](https://buildingregs.app/privacy)) |
| 4 | Look up the company on Companies House | Several tools name no company |
| 5 | Ask for a certificate with a number (Cyber Essentials, ISO 27001), and check the register | Claims are rarely shown with numbers ([Struck](https://struck.eu/uk)) |
| 6 | Strip names, addresses, phone numbers, bank details, health details, passwords and confidential tender papers before pasting | NCSC advice ([NCSC](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/blog-post/chatgpt-and-large-language-models-whats-the-risk)) |
| 7 | Check every answer against the source; prefer tools that show the clause, and treat "0% hallucination" or "fully compliant" as a warning | No tool publishes accuracy; terms put risk on you |
| 8 | Keep a competent person signing RAMS, quotes and compliance papers | MHSWR reg 3 ([Ramsgen](https://www.ramsgen.com)) |
| 9 | For AI receptionists and agents, tell callers about AI and recording, keep human approval, and know how to switch it off | Consent left to you ([Jobber](https://help.getjobber.com/hc/en-us/articles/25315927533847-Receptionist-powered-by-Jobber-AI)); NCSC agentic guidance ([Bratby Law](https://bratby.law/ncsc-agentic-ai-guidance/)) |
| 10 | Write a one-page AI rule for staff: which tools are allowed, what never goes in | Most use is admin by staff ([Specification Online](https://specificationonline.co.uk/sections/sustainability/articles/2026-11-11/ciob/builders-embrace-ai-as-pressures-mount-across-construction)) |

Several points remain unverified: no certificate was checked against a register; Procore's AI terms and accuracy document are gated; Struck, StructureGPT and Sleepless Tradesman terms could not be read; QuickBooks security certificates, Autodesk Construction Cloud's certificates and residency, and OpenAI's ISO 42001 claim were not confirmed on primary pages; and the reported Copilot default for Claude in UK tenants comes from secondary sources that disagree.

## Conclusion

The honest picture for the room is not "AI tools are unsafe". It is that trust signals are uneven and the vendor's paperwork, not the homepage, tells the truth. A small UK tool with a named AI provider, UK hosting, a DPA and a citation on every answer can be more transparent than a famous app that added AI without updating its privacy policy. And every vendor, large or small, quietly agrees on one thing in its terms: the builder signs off and carries the risk. So the useful question is not "is this tool compliant?" but "can this tool show me where my data goes, and can I check its answer in two minutes?"

The same test applies to anything builders_lab builds. To earn trust in this market, a new tool would need to do what no one here does yet: publish its test questions and error rate, name its AI provider and data location, offer a DPA from day one, and make no absolute claims. That is a low bar in principle, and an open gap in practice.
