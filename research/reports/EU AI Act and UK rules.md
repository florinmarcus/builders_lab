# Build for UK data law, design for EU labels

Checked 18 Nov 2026. Not legal advice. "Interpretation" marks our reading of the law, not a source's words. "Unverified" marks a claim from one secondary source, a search snippet or background knowledge that was not re-read this session.

For the pilot in England, the EU AI Act very likely does not apply to builders_lab, and the UK has no AI law to replace it. What binds us now is UK data protection law, the council's contract and data terms, and ordinary safety and liability law. The EU Act itself is live but changed: the **Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744), in force 27 Jul 2026, moved the high-risk duties to 2 Dec 2027 (Annex III uses) and 2 Aug 2028 (Annex I products)**, while the transparency duties in Article 50 still started on **2 Aug 2026** ([EUR-Lex, Reg. 2026/1744](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng)). Interpretation: a RAG assistant that reads a contractor's council emails and answers in Romanian with citations is not high-risk under Annex III. If we serve firms in Romania, we become a non-EU provider placing an AI system on the EU market. We must then tell users they are talking to an AI, mark generated text in a machine-readable way unless an exemption fits, and support AI literacy. That is cheap to build now and costly to retrofit, so the product should carry it from day one. In the UK, the hard part is the data chain: the council is controller of tenant data, the contractor is usually its processor, and we would be a sub-processor who may need the council's written approval. On safety, the law is clear that the asbestos and CDM duties stay with the contractor. HSE said on **12 Jun 2026** that existing law applies to AI "regardless of the technology being used" ([HSE](https://press.hse.gov.uk/2026/06/12/hses-regulatory-approach-to-artificial-intelligence-ai/)). The assistant must behave like a careful document finder, never like a surveyor.

## The EU delayed high-risk rules but kept transparency on time

The early AI Act dates all held. The Act entered into force on 1 Aug 2024. Prohibited practices and the AI literacy duty applied from 2 Feb 2025. Rules for general-purpose AI (GPAI) models, such as Claude, and the penalty framework applied from 2 Aug 2025 ([Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/)). The big change came in 2026. The Digital Omnibus on AI was **adopted, not just proposed**: political agreement on 6–7 May 2026, signed 8 Jul 2026, published 24 Jul 2026, in force 27 Jul 2026 ([EUR-Lex, Reg. 2026/1744](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng); [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/)). Secondary sources disagree on some steps (one gives Council approval on 29 Jun 2026), so this report uses the EUR-Lex dates.

| EU AI Act item | Date it applies (as of 18 Nov 2026) | Changed by the Omnibus? |
|---|---|---|
| Entry into force | 1 Aug 2024 | No |
| Prohibited practices, AI literacy (Art 4) | 2 Feb 2025 | Art 4 softened (see below) |
| GPAI model rules, governance, penalties | 2 Aug 2025 | No |
| Transparency (Art 50): AI notice, deepfake and public-interest text labels | 2 Aug 2026 | No |
| Art 50(2) machine marking, for generative systems already on the market before 2 Aug 2026 | 2 Dec 2026 | New grace period |
| New prohibition: AI intimate imagery without consent, CSAM | 2 Dec 2026 | New |
| High-risk, Annex III uses (jobs, credit, essential services, etc.) | 2 Dec 2027 (was 2 Aug 2026) | Delayed |
| High-risk, Annex I products (machinery, lifts, toys, etc.) | 2 Aug 2028 (was 2 Aug 2027) | Delayed |
| National AI sandbox in each member state | 2 Aug 2027 (was 2 Aug 2026) | Delayed |

Sources: [EUR-Lex, Reg. 2026/1744](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng); [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/); [Kinstellar](https://www.kinstellar.com/news-and-insights/detail/4619/the-ai-act-after-the-digital-omnibus-simplified-rules-delayed-deadlines-but-can-compliance-wait).

The grace period matters for us only in one way: it covers systems already on the market before 2 Aug 2026. Interpretation: a product launched in the EU now gets **no grace period** for machine marking. The Omnibus also softened Article 4. Providers and deployers must now "take measures to support the development of AI literacy of their staff", instead of ensuring "a sufficient level" ([EUR-Lex, Reg. 2026/1744](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng)). The Commission had proposed removing the duty; the final text kept a lighter version ([Kinstellar](https://www.kinstellar.com/news-and-insights/detail/4619/the-ai-act-after-the-digital-omnibus-simplified-rules-delayed-deadlines-but-can-compliance-wait)).

The guidance around Article 50 is now final. The Code of Practice on marking and labelling AI content was published on 10 Jun 2026 ([Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/06/european-commission-publishes-final-code-of-practice-on-marking-and-labelling-aigenerated-content)). The Commission found it adequate on 8 Jul 2026 and published final Article 50 guidelines on 20 Jul 2026 ([Faegre Drinker](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2026/7/eu-ai-act-commission-confirms-transparency-code-of-practice-as-adequate-and-publishes-final-version-of-its-guidelines-on-transparency-obligations)). Four points from the law-firm summary matter to us. AI agents must disclose that they are AI and on whose behalf they act. Plain AI translation now counts as "standard editing" and needs no marking, but summaries and substantive rewrites still do. Marking is simplified where free text cannot carry metadata. And a **B2B carve-out** lets providers skip marking where outputs stay inside closed business environments with safeguards such as tenant isolation and role-based access, but not for public or consumer systems ([Faegre Drinker](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2026/7/eu-ai-act-commission-confirms-transparency-code-of-practice-as-adequate-and-publishes-final-version-of-its-guidelines-on-transparency-obligations)). Unverified: we read only summaries, not the guidelines text. The guidelines on what counts as high-risk are still a draft (19 May 2026), with the final version promised by the end of 2026 ([Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/draft-guidelines-high-risk)).

## builders_lab is a limited-risk system, and only once it reaches the EU

Interpretation: none of the Annex III areas fits "find what my council emails and surveys say". Annex III covers biometrics, safety components of critical infrastructure, education, employment and worker management, access to essential services and credit, law enforcement, migration and justice ([Annex III](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/annex/3/)). Annex I lists EU product-safety laws such as machinery, lifts and toys ([Annex I](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/annex/1/)). Interpretation: buildings and construction products are not in Annex I, so the "safety component of a product" route does not apply. The risk lies in features we might add later. A feature that ranks, monitors or allocates work to workers would fall under employment (Annex III point 4). A feature that decides tenants' access to services could fall under essential services. The draft guidelines warn that excluding high-risk uses in the terms of service alone is not enough ([William Fry](https://www.williamfry.com/knowledge/eu-publishes-draft-guidelines-on-classification-of-high-risk-ai-systems/)), so the product must not be able to do these things, not just forbid them.

Roles under the Act, interpretation: builders_lab is the **provider** of the AI system, because we build it and offer it under our name. Each building firm is a **deployer**. Anthropic is the **GPAI model provider** for Claude, and AWS distributes the model through Bedrock. Anthropic's GPAI duties have applied since 2 Aug 2025 ([Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/)). We take on no GPAI-model duties unless we substantially modify the model, which is not planned. Because we and Anthropic are different companies, our system would be supervised by national authorities, not the EU AI Office ([EUR-Lex, Reg. 2026/1744](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng)).

The territorial question decides everything. Article 2 covers providers placing systems on the EU market wherever they are based, deployers in the EU, and non-EU providers and deployers where "the output produced by the AI system is used in the Union" ([AI Act Service Desk, Art 2](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-2)). Commentators note the output trigger has no intent test, so it reads broadly ([William Fry](https://www.williamfry.com/knowledge/a-practical-guide-to-the-extraterritorial-reach-of-the-ai-act/)). Interpretation: UK firms using the tool in England, for English council work, are very likely outside the Act. The edge case is a UK firm sending AI-written text to someone in the EU, such as a Romanian supplier. Serving firms in Romania clearly brings us in. Then our duties as provider are Article 50(1) (tell people at first interaction that they are dealing with an AI, unless obvious) and Article 50(2) (machine-readable marking of generated text, unless the editing exemption or the B2B carve-out fits), plus Article 4 ([Art 50](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/)). Article 50(4) applies to deployers who publish AI text to inform the public on matters of public interest; interpretation: internal contractor correspondence is not that. We would **not need an EU authorised representative**, which Article 22 requires only for high-risk systems ([Art 22](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/22/)).

Penalties for breaking Article 50 sit in the middle tier: up to EUR 15m or 3% of worldwide turnover, and for SMEs whichever is lower ([Art 99](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/99/)). Romania named **ANCOM** as market surveillance authority and single point of contact by government memorandum on 12 Mar 2026, with ANSPDCP (data protection) and others in sector roles. The national implementing law was still being prepared in July 2026 ([Kinstellar](https://www.kinstellar.com/news-and-insights/detail/4619/the-ai-act-after-the-digital-omnibus-simplified-rules-delayed-deadlines-but-can-compliance-wait); [Chambers Romania 2026](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/artificial-intelligence-2026/romania)). One commentator says ANCOM cannot sanction until that law takes effect ([The AI Act Guy](https://theaiactguy.substack.com/p/who-enforces-the-ai-act-29)). Unverified: whether the Romanian law was adopted between July and November 2026. Interpretation: near-term enforcement against a small limited-risk tool is unlikely, but the duties apply directly, so we build them anyway.

A second EU law matters more for liability than the AI Act does. The new **Product Liability Directive (EU) 2024/2853 treats software, including standalone software, as a product**, with transposition due by 9 Dec 2026 ([Faegre Drinker, Oct 2026](https://www.faegredrinker.com/en/insights/publications/2026/10/transposing-the-eu-new-product-liability-directive-a-member-state-progress-report-october-2026-update)). Romania had not published its transposition as of July–August 2026 ([Global Law Experts](https://globallawexperts.com/eu-product-liability-directive-2024-2853-what-romanian-tech-businesses-must-know-about-software-as-a-product/); [Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/articles/eu-product-liability-directive-where-things-stand-with-member-state-transposition/)). Interpretation: once in force, an injured person in Romania could claim against the software supplier directly for a defect, without proving negligence.

## The UK has no AI law; data protection and council contracts do the work

As of Nov 2026 the UK still governs AI through existing laws and regulators, following the 2023 white paper's five principles (safety, transparency, fairness, accountability, contestability) ([GOV.UK white paper](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-regulation-a-pro-innovation-approach/white-paper)). No AI bill was before Parliament as of May 2026 ([Bratby Law](https://bratby.law/uk-ai-regulation-what-the-law-says/)). Unverified (commentary only): the King's Speech of 13 May 2026 had no AI bill ([io-sphere](https://io-sphere.io/guides/ai-regulation-uk-2026)), and one firm expects the next King's Speech "not ... until late 2027 at the earliest" ([Lewis Silkin, 14 Sep 2026](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/09/14/ai-judgment-day-on-the-horizon-while-uk-lawmakers-play-catch-up)). Copyright reform was shelved: on 18 Mar 2026 the government dropped its preferred text-and-data-mining exception, and on 15 May 2026 said it has no current plans to change copyright law ([HCWS1416](https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-statements/detail/2026-03-18/hcws1416); [Lords committee](https://committees.parliament.uk/committee/170/communications-and-digital-committee/news/213575/committee-publishes-government-response-to-ai-and-copyright-report/)). Interpretation: this barely touches us, because we do not train models; we retrieve from the contractor's own documents and must not republish them. The Cyber Security and Resilience Bill (Lords third reading 2 Nov 2026, not yet law) brings medium and large managed service providers into scope ([Resultsense, 12 Nov 2026](https://www.resultsense.com/insights/2026-11-12-rewiring-the-state-ai-civil-service/)); interpretation: a tiny start-up is unlikely to be caught at launch.

Data protection is the law that binds us most. The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 brought its main changes into force on **5 Feb 2026**, including new UK GDPR Articles 22A–22D. Solely automated decisions with significant effects moved from "prohibited unless permitted" to "permitted with safeguards" (information, human review, a right to contest), with stricter rules for special category data ([LexisNexis](https://www.lexisnexis.co.uk/legal/news/key-provisions-of-data-use-access-act-2025-to-come-into-force-on-5-february-2026); [Travers Smith](https://www.traverssmith.com/knowledge/knowledge-container/uks-data-protection-reforms-take-effect-a-new-era-for-automated-decision-making/)). Since 12 May 2026 the regulator must write a statutory code on AI and automated decisions; no draft is out, and one firm expects it in 2027 ([Bratby Law](https://bratby.law/ico-ai-biometrics-code-of-practice/)). Unverified (one source): the ICO's duties passed to the new Information Commission on 30 Sep 2026 ([Bratby Law](https://bratby.law/uk-ai-regulation-what-the-law-says/)). Interpretation: builders_lab finds and explains documents; it does not decide anything about tenants, so Articles 22A–22D should not bite, as long as it never auto-decides access refusals, charges or priorities.

The real risk is the processor chain. UK GDPR Article 28 needs a written processor contract, bars a processor from adding a sub-processor without the controller's prior written authorisation, and keeps the processor liable for its sub-processors (unverified: not re-read this session) ([UK GDPR Art 28](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/28)). Interpretation: the council is controller of tenant data; the contractor is usually its processor; builders_lab would be the contractor's processor, and AWS ours. So the contractor may need the council's written consent before any council data enters our system. Emails about repairs can carry health and disability details, which are special category data. A DPIA is expected for new technology like AI (unverified) ([ICO DPIA guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/accountability-and-governance/data-protection-impact-assessments-dpias/)). Our earlier cost work found that Bedrock in London keeps stored data in the UK, and that EU-profile inference stays in the UK and EU but may leave the UK ([AWS ML blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/unlocking-ai-flexibility-in-europe-a-guide-to-cross-region-inference-for-eu-data-processing-and-model-access/); [Bedrock FAQs](https://aws.amazon.com/bedrock/faqs/)). The DPIA and the council approval must say this plainly.

Public-sector AI rules mostly bind councils, not their contractors. The Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard (ATRS) is mandatory for central government departments and some arm's-length bodies, and only recommended for local government ([GOV.UK ATRS hub](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/algorithmic-transparency-recording-standard-hub); [Data and AI Ethics Framework](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/data-ethics-framework/data-and-ai-ethics-framework)). The GDS AI Playbook (10 Feb 2025) is guidance for public bodies ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-playbook-for-the-uk-government)). Procurement note PPN 017 asks bidders to disclose AI use in bids; the answers are for information and not scored ([Civil Service Procurement Pathway](https://www.procurementpathway.civilservice.gov.uk/documents/ppn/ppn-017-improving-transparency-of-ai-use-in-procurement/strategy-and-plan)). Unverified: whether councils are formally bound by PPNs. What matters in practice is that councils are checking. The LGA told Parliament that many councils are auditing suppliers for AI use and **30% of respondents already had a policy or contract clauses requiring suppliers to declare it** ([LGA evidence UAIG0036](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/135021/pdf/)). Councils also carry the Public Sector Equality Duty ([Equality Act s149](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/15/section/149)). Interpretation: a tool that drops a tenant's vulnerability flag could harm the council's compliance, so the assistant must surface such flags, not summarise them away. No sample council AI clause was found; that is the biggest gap in this section.

## Safety duties stay with the contractor whatever the software says

UK safety law is goal-setting, and the AI question does not change who holds the duty. Regulation 5 of the Control of Asbestos Regulations 2012 says an employer must not start work liable to expose employees to asbestos unless it has made "a suitable and sufficient assessment" of whether asbestos is present, or assumes it is present and not chrysotile alone ([legislation.gov.uk reg 5](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/632/regulation/5)). HSE's AI policy statement of 12 Jun 2026 says the law applies "regardless of the technology being used", that "those who create risks are best placed to manage and control that risk", and that any AI use affecting workplace safety needs a risk assessment. HSE promised new AI guidance for 2026/27; we found none published by Nov 2026 ([HSE](https://press.hse.gov.uk/2026/06/12/hses-regulatory-approach-to-artificial-intelligence-ai/)). We found no HSE statement on AI-written RAMS and no prosecution involving an AI tool. A trade body warns that safety duties "cannot be delegated to automated systems" and that AI RAMS tend to be generic ([Building Safety Group](https://bsg.barques.dev/news/dangers-of-using-ai-to-create-risk-assessments-and-method-statements/); unverified, the page sits on a staging-like domain). A 2026 pilot study found no credible evidence that AI matches workers' own point-of-work risk assessments ([CIB/Purdue](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/cib-conferences/vol2/iss1/24)). CDM 2015 duties were not re-read from the primary text this session ([SI 2015/51](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2015/51/contents)); our earlier work found that the CDM client must give contractors project-specific asbestos information ([HSG264](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/priced/hsg264.pdf)).

The Building Safety Act's golden thread mostly does not reach our pilot. It applies to higher-risk buildings: at least 18 m or 7 storeys, with at least 2 homes ([GOV.UK HRB criteria](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/criteria-for-being-a-higher-risk-building-during-the-occupation-phase-of-the-new-higher-risk-regime.)). Interpretation: most council low-rise flats are outside it, but council tower blocks are in scope, and if we ever cover them the golden thread is a structured, dated source owned by the council. Its idea of accurate, current, traceable information is a good standard for our answers. We found no Building Safety Regulator statement on AI.

Professional bodies agree that AI assists and people stay accountable. The **RICS standard on responsible AI use has been mandatory since 9 Mar 2026** for all members and regulated firms: "The surveyor remains accountable for every piece of professional advice, regardless of the tools used" ([RICS](https://www.rics.org/news-insights/rics-first-ever-standard-on-responsible-ai-use-now-in-effect)). It asks for an AI risk register, a use policy, supplier checks, validation of outputs and openness with clients. Interpretation: our contractors are rarely RICS members, but the asbestos and stock surveys we read often come from RICS-regulated firms, and councils will recognise these principles. CIOB has a free AI playbook (2024) and RIBA reports practice-level AI use up from 41% in 2024 to 59% in 2025 ([CIOB](https://www.ciob.org/news/new-playbook-on-ai-in-construction-published-by-ciob); [RIBA](https://www.riba.org/knowledge-and-resources/knowledge-landing-page/second-riba-ai-report-shows-surge-in-usage-among-uk-architects)).

Liability follows the same line. Interpretation: after a wrong asbestos answer, the contractor faces HSE and claims from workers or residents, and may then try to recover from us. Our customers are businesses, so the Consumer Rights Act mostly does not apply. Under the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977, we can cap ordinary losses only if the term is reasonable, and **we cannot exclude liability for death or personal injury caused by negligence** ([UCTA s2](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1977/50/section/2)). Asbestos harm is personal injury, often decades later. The Law Commission is consulting until 14 Jan 2027 on bringing software and AI inside the Consumer Protection Act 1987, with a rebuttable presumption of defect for hard AI cases; final recommendations are expected in late 2027 ([Kennedys](https://www.kennedyslaw.com/en/thought-leadership/article/2026/law-commission-consultation-signals-possible-major-changes-to-the-product-liability-regime-in-england-and-wales/)). This is a proposal, not law. On insurance, US liability forms added generative AI exclusions from Jan 2026, while UK PI insurers mostly have not yet added dedicated AI exclusions ([Gallagher](https://www.ajg.com/us/news-and-insights/2026/jul/iso-introduces-generative-ai-exclusion-in-commercial-general-liability-policies/); [CMS](https://cms.law/en/media/international/files/events/webinars/ai-in-construction-projects-executive-summary)). Interpretation: disclaimers help show the tool was not sold as a survey, but product design is the real control.

Romania follows the same core rule through EU law. Worker protection from asbestos sits in HG 1875/2005, and the Labour Inspectorate asks for a written risk assessment and work plan per task, and tells workers to stop work and report if they find unexpected asbestos ([Inspecția Muncii, worker guide](https://www.inspectiamuncii.ro/documents/825609/1209953/Ghid+azbest_lucrator.pdf/e52fa964-0c59-4777-a671-035d78cb5aef); [employer guide](https://www.inspectiamuncii.ro/documents/825609/1209953/Ghid+azbest_angajator.pdf/572f16c5-2598-455d-b054-64751e0cd72f)). A draft to transpose the 2023 asbestos directive existed in July 2025 ([CES draft](https://www.ces.ro/newlib/PDF/proiecte/2025/NF-proiect-transp-Dir-2023_2668-azbest_DPP_EP_16-iul-2025-curat.pdf)); unverified whether it was adopted. Interpretation: "assume present when unclear, stop and check" works in both countries.

## Two regimes, one product that satisfies both

| Topic (as of 18 Nov 2026) | UK | EU AI Act |
|---|---|---|
| Legal form | No AI Act. Existing laws and regulators, 2023 white paper principles | Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, amended by 2026/1744 |
| Risk classes | None. UK GDPR Arts 22A–22D for automated decisions (from 5 Feb 2026) | Prohibited, high-risk, transparency (Art 50), minimal; GPAI rules |
| Telling users it is AI | No general legal duty; white paper transparency principle; PPN 017 bid disclosure (info only) | Required since 2 Aug 2026 (Art 50(1)) |
| Marking generated text | No duty | Required since 2 Aug 2026 for new systems (Art 50(2)), with exemptions |
| Staff AI literacy | No duty | "Support" AI literacy, since 2 Feb 2025, softened Jul 2026 |
| Upcoming | ICO/Information Commission AI code (expected 2027, unverified); HSE AI guidance 2026/27; Law Commission product liability (to 14 Jan 2027) | Final high-risk guidelines (end 2026); high-risk duties 2 Dec 2027 / 2 Aug 2028; PLD transposition 9 Dec 2026 |
| Fines | No AI fines. UK GDPR up to £17.5m or 4% (unverified) | Art 50: up to EUR 15m or 3%, lower of the two for SMEs |
| Regulator | ICO (Information Commission), HSE, sector regulators | ANCOM in Romania (law pending), EU AI Office for GPAI |
| For our pilot | Applies now through data, contract and safety law | Very likely does not apply to UK-only use (interpretation) |

Sources: as cited in the sections above; [Bratby Law](https://bratby.law/uk-ai-regulation-what-the-law-says/); [Art 99](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/99/).

The gap between the two is smaller in practice than on paper. The EU asks for an AI notice, marking and literacy. UK councils and the ICO ask for a DPIA, a clear data chain and human review. HSE and RICS ask for human accountability and a risk assessment of the tool. One design covers all of them.

## Design rules for the product

These are proposals reasoned from the duties above, not legal requirements. A solicitor and an asbestos professional should review them before real use.

| # | Rule | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Show "You are talking to an AI assistant (Claude, via builders_lab)" in Romanian and English at first use, and on every exported text | EU Art 50(1); UK transparency principle; council AI declarations |
| 2 | Add a visible "AI-generated" footer and a machine-readable marker (metadata or header) to every generated summary, email draft and export | EU Art 50(2); no grace period for a new launch |
| 3 | Cite the source in every answer: document, page or section, date, and for asbestos the survey type and rooms covered. Show the English source text next to the Romanian answer | Reg 5 assessment stays with the contractor; reduces mistranslation risk; golden-thread standard |
| 4 | Never say "there is no asbestos". Say what the survey found and what it did not cover. If not covered or unclear: "Treat as containing asbestos until checked (reg 5)". If the user reports suspect material: "Stop work and get it checked" | CAR 2012 reg 5(b); Romanian Labour Inspectorate guide |
| 5 | Flag when only a management survey exists for refurbishment work, and when a survey is old | HSG264 survey types |
| 6 | Never produce a finished or signed RAMS, risk assessment or survey. Drafts carry "needs competent person review" | HSWA/CDM duties; HSE AI statement; RICS principle |
| 7 | No decisions about tenants or workers: no ranking, scoring, monitoring or allocating people; no access refusals or charges | Keeps us out of EU Annex III and UK GDPR Arts 22A–22D |
| 8 | Surface tenant vulnerability and access notes accurately; never drop them from a summary | Council PSED; Equality Act reasonable adjustments |
| 9 | Process and store in AWS London; keep inference on the EU profile; no model training on customer data; per-firm isolation and role-based access | UK GDPR Art 28 and council data terms; EU B2B carve-out conditions |
| 10 | Log question, answer, sources, model version, user and time; delete per property and at contract end | Shows what was relied on; council audit and deletion terms |
| 11 | Ship a one-page "how to use this tool safely" guide in Romanian and English | EU Art 4 literacy; HSE risk assessment of AI use |
| 12 | Evals include negative tests: room not covered, old survey, conflicting documents, where the right answer is "not covered: assume present" | Turns rule 4 into a tested behaviour |

## What we still do not know

Several points need checking before the presentation relies on them. We have not read the primary Article 50 guidelines or Code, only law-firm summaries, so the B2B carve-out and the translation exemption are unconfirmed. We have no sample of real council AI or data clauses; two or three public tender packs from Find a Tender would fix that. Whether Romania adopted its AI Act implementing law, its PLD law or its asbestos amendment between July and November 2026 is unknown. The ICO-to-Information Commission handover date, the King's Speech contents and the Lord Holmes bill lapse rest on single or secondary sources. No UK case law exists on a contractor relying on an AI summary, and negligent misstatement by an information tool supplier needs a lawyer's view.

## Conclusion

The useful finding is that regulation does not block the pilot, but it does shape the product. The EU Act asks for little from a limited-risk tool, and the UK asks for no AI-specific steps at all. The real gate is commercial and contractual: a council must agree to its tenant data reaching us. So the first compliance task is not an AI Act file but a short data pack the contractor can hand to the council: processor terms, DPIA, data location and an AI-use statement.

The safety rules point the same way. Every regime we looked at, from CAR 2012 to RICS to the Romanian Labour Inspectorate, keeps the human duty-holder in charge. That makes the safest design also the most sellable one: a tool that shows its source, admits what the survey did not cover, and says "assume asbestos" when in doubt. Building EU labels in now costs a few days and removes a later redesign for Romania. The main future risk is not the AI Act but product liability, if the UK and Romania start treating software as a product.
