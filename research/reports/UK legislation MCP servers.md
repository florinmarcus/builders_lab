# Many servers fetch UK law, none know the building rules

*Research report, 21 Nov 2026. Based on four sets of research notes made on that date (web pages, GitHub, the official MCP registry, and live calls to five hosted servers).*

At least a dozen MCP servers give an AI access to UK legislation. Almost all are free, open source wrappers around the free legislation.gov.uk API. None of them covers the Approved Documents as searchable text. The best are The National Archives' own **legislation-mcp-ts** (OGL, self-host only), **cyanheads/uk-legislation-mcp-server** (Apache-2.0, careful with dates and versions), **paulieb89/uk-legal-mcp** (MIT, the widest and most used) and the government's **i.AI Lex** (MIT code, OGL data, the only one with meaning-based search, but labelled "experimental"). Because they pass any law reference through to legislation.gov.uk, they all reach the Building Regulations 2010, the Building Safety Act, CDM 2015, the Control of Asbestos Regulations and the Construction Act. But they do not know those laws matter more than any other. The commercial legal services (Thomson Reuters CoCounsel, Harvey, Legora) do ship MCP servers. They need a paid seat per user, cost hundreds of dollars a month (reported US prices), and Thomson Reuters' terms ban using its content as a knowledge source next to other AI tools. No construction body (NBS, BSI, NHBC, LABC) offers an API or MCP server. The only Approved Documents MCP server is a hobby project built on an out of date PDF. For builders_lab this means: borrow ideas and code from the open source legislation servers, ignore the commercial ones, and keep building your own Approved Documents and HSE guidance corpus. Nobody else fills that gap.

## Twelve free servers wrap the same free government API

The source of almost everything is the legislation.gov.uk API. It is free and needs no key. You add `/data.xml`, `/data.akn` or `/data.html` to any law's address. Content is under the Open Government Licence v3.0. The fair use limit is **1,500 requests in any 5 minutes**, and callers must send a real User-Agent ([legislation.gov.uk fair use](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/fair-use-policy); [developer page](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/developer)). An older GitHub page says 3,000; treat the official 1,500 as current. legislation.gov.uk itself runs no hosted MCP server.

The servers fall into three groups. The first group fetches live from legislation.gov.uk and can read a law as it stood on a given date. The second group keeps its own copy and index. The third group is toys or narrow tools. The table covers the ones worth knowing. Stars and dates are GitHub figures from 21 Nov 2026.

The National Archives' server is the most "official" of the group. It sits in the same GitHub org as the legislation.gov.uk data docs and is under the OGL. But no press release was found, so "official" rests on the org and the licence. It is self-host only. Its tools cover search, full text, single sections, table of contents, amendments ("effects") and metadata, and it ships short guides on reading a past version and checking outstanding changes ([README](https://github.com/legislation/legislation-mcp-ts)).

Lex is the only government-run hosted server. It holds **220,000 Acts and SIs** ("complete from 1963"), 892,000 amendments and 89,000 explanatory notes. Its case law is "temporarily disabled pending TNA licence" ([Lex README](https://github.com/i-dot-ai/lex)). It says plainly it is for "research and non-production use" and must not be a production dependency ([Lex site](https://lex.lab.i.ai.gov.uk/)). In one live test on 21 Nov 2026, a search for "means of escape in case of fire building regulations" put **a 1913 local Act first**, with text that had been machine-read by a model. The Building Regulations 2010 did not come first. One query is an anecdote, not a measure. But it shows the risk of meaning-based search over all UK law with no focus on building.

The rest are small. The highest star count for any UK-law MCP server is 21. No independent tests or reviews of any of them were found. Quality judgments here come from their own READMEs and a few live calls.

## Every wrapper reaches the building laws, but none treats them as special

All the live wrappers pass any law type, year and number through to legislation.gov.uk. So they all reach the Building Regulations 2010 (SI 2010/2214), the Building Safety Act 2022, CDM 2015, the Control of Asbestos Regulations 2012, the Housing Grants, Construction and Regeneration Act 1996 and the Scheme for Construction Contracts. Only the Building Regulations were tested live. On cyanheads, a request for regulation 7 as at 1 Jan 2020 returned the text in force from the nearest earlier version, listed every version up to one dated 20 Dec 2025, and showed "outstanding effects 0" with a warning that revised text can lag ([cyanheads README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cyanheads/uk-legislation-mcp-server/main/README.md)). The other laws were not tested. The exact SI numbers for CDM, asbestos and the Scheme came from memory in the notes and need checking.

Two exceptions matter. Ansvar indexes only about 3,241 Acts, so it very likely misses the Building Regulations, CDM and the asbestos rules, which are SIs (an inference from its own description). The narrow "uk-legislation-changes" server covers just 528 chosen provisions ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/busybusybussiness/uk-legislation-changes/main/README.md)).

The real quality problems sit upstream, in legislation.gov.uk, and every wrapper inherits them. First, if you ask for a date, the site **silently returns the nearest earlier version** while echoing back the date you asked for. Pipeworx documents this and returns the true "valid from" date on every answer ([Pipeworx README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pipeworx-io/mcp-legislation-uk/main/README.md)). Second, revised text lags behind new amendments. Each law shows an "up to date with all changes known to be in force on or before" date and a list of outstanding changes ([legislation.gov.uk changes](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/changes)). Third, the site's firewall blocks some hosted servers, so paulieb89 and openlaw both advise running locally ([paulieb89 README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/paulieb89/uk-legal-mcp/main/README.md); [openlaw README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/damankaur-dev/openlaw-mcp/main/README.md)). Shared hosted servers also crawl at about one request a second. A tool for builders must read the "up to date to" date and the outstanding changes and pass them on. Most servers expose this data; only some put it in front of the model clearly.

Case law is a separate question. Judgments on Find Case Law are under the Open Justice Licence, which **does not allow computational analysis**. Bulk search or extraction needs a separate free licence, assessed case by case ([Find Case Law re-use](https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/re-use-find-case-law-records)). That is why Lex has switched its case law off.

## No server has the Approved Documents as searchable text

The Approved Documents are guidance, not law. They live on gov.uk, published by MHCLG under OGL v3.0, as **PDF only**. Approved Document B alone is two PDFs of 200 and 224 pages, last changed on 16 Sep 2026 for second stairs and evacuation lifts in tall residential buildings ([gov.uk Content API, AD B](https://www.gov.uk/api/content/government/publications/fire-safety-approved-document-b)). The collection lists different dates for each part: B in Sep 2026, T in Nov 2025, Q in Nov 2024, G and M in Oct 2024, and A, C and K still on 2013 ([gov.uk collection](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/approved-documents)). New Approved Documents L and F came with SI 2026/335 and Circular 01/2026, and apply from 24 Mar 2027 ([Circular 01/2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-future-homes-and-buildings-standards-building-circular-012026/the-future-homes-and-buildings-standards-building-circular-012026-letter); [HBF](https://www.hbf.co.uk/news/future-homes-standard-april-2026/)).

GOV.UK has a free, keyless Content API that anyone can use "for any purpose" ([Content API docs](https://content-api.publishing.service.gov.uk/)). It gives page details and PDF links, not the PDF text. GDS has no official MCP server. Community GOV.UK servers exist. In a live test, **paulieb89/govuk-mcp** (MIT, hosted) found the Approved Document B page and its FAQ ([README](https://github.com/paulieb89/govuk-mcp)). But these servers return page details and links. They do not parse the PDFs. So they can tell a model where Approved Document B is. They cannot answer what paragraph 2.5 says.

The only server built for the Approved Documents is **LeoRickayzen/uk-building-code-mcp**. It has 0 stars, no licence, and its last push was Nov 2025. It runs vector search over the October 2024 merged Approved Documents PDF and says it is "not recommended for production use" ([GitHub](https://github.com/LeoRickayzen/uk-building-code-mcp)). That merged PDF predates the 2025 and 2026 changes to Parts B, L and F ([gov.uk index](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/building-regulations-and-approved-documents-index)). So it would give old answers on fire safety and energy.

HSE guidance is in the same position. HSE web content is under OGL v3.0, with a few exceptions such as the Law poster ([HSE copyright](https://www.hse.gov.uk/help/copyright.htm)). HSE has no API or MCP server. Whether the priced HSE Books titles, such as L153 for CDM and L143 for asbestos, are fully under the OGL when downloaded free from hse.gov.uk was not confirmed per title.

Abroad, the closest models are a Canadian building code MCP server with 24,000+ sections that makes users bring their own PDF to avoid copyright problems ([GitHub](https://github.com/DavidCho1999/Canada_building_code_mcp)), and a Japanese one built on the government e-Gov API ([GitHub](https://github.com/Sora-bluesky/building-standards-act-mcp)). Legis-Link offers trade standards (such as BS 7671) for several countries, including the UK, behind a free and a paid tier. It covers trade standards, not the Building Regulations ([GitHub](https://github.com/eutech-directory/legis-link-mcp)).

## Paid legal and construction content is closed to an assistant like this

Three commercial legal firms ship official MCP servers: Thomson Reuters (CoCounsel Legal, over Westlaw and Practical Law), Harvey and Legora. Each needs a paid account, and each user signs in with their own ([TR help page](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en-us/help/cocounsel/legal/integrations/mcp-connector/cocounsel-legal-mcp-connector); [Harvey docs](https://developers.harvey.ai/guides/harvey_mcp)). Thomson Reuters calls its connector a pilot it may withdraw at any time. Its terms say the content may not be "used as a retrieval or knowledge source for AI systems" or passed to other tools, and users must not turn on "always allow" ([TR help page](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en-us/help/cocounsel/legal/integrations/mcp-connector/cocounsel-legal-mcp-connector)). That is exactly what happens when a builder runs it in the same Claude session as another connector. LexisNexis has no official Claude connector. It went the other way and put Claude inside Lexis+ with Protégé ([The Legal Wire](https://thelegalwire.ai/lexisnexis-expands-lexis-with-protege-by-integrating-anthropics-claude-legal-plugin-suite/)). vLex/Vincent has no official Claude MCP server.

No UK prices are published. **Reported US prices** are about $784 per user per month for CoCounsel Legal, $257 to $400 for Westlaw Advantage, and about $128 to $494 for Lexis+ with Protégé. These come from a competitor's blog, not the vendors ([cases.ai](https://cases.ai/lexisnexis-claude-connector)). UK public sector contracts give a sense of scale: the Met Office paid about **£40,870 ex VAT for one year** of Practical Law plus Westlaw ([Find a Tender](https://www.find-tender.service.gov.uk/Notice/018722-2025)). Practical Law Construction does have notes on building regulations and the Building Safety Act ([Practical Law UK](https://uk.practicallaw.thomsonreuters.com/0-381-0634?transitionType=Default&contextData=%28sc.Default%29)). They are written for lawyers, about contracts and liability, not for a builder asking what to do on site.

Construction content holders offer no machine access at all. NBS sells the Approved Documents and the standards they cite through its Construction Information Service, with no public price and no API found ([NBS CIS](https://www.thenbs.com/products/construction-information-service)). BSI has no AI licence type and no public prices. Its standard terms ban storing standards "in a retrieval system" and making "derivative works" without written permission ([BSI terms](https://knowledge.bsigroup.com/pages/terms-and-conditions-of-supply)). That matters because the Approved Documents point to almost 500 standards ([Kruiper et al., Strathclyde](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/89810/1/Kruiper-etal-AEI-2024-A-platform-based-Natural-Language-processing-driven-strategy-for-digitalising-regulatory-compliance-processes.pdf)). NHBC and LABC showed no AI, API or MCP offer.

On the government side, things may change. GDS and The National Archives ran a project in 2026 on making legislation and case law "AI ready". A hackathon found that "MCP makes a significant positive difference to the quality of AI outputs", and the work moved to Alpha in June 2026 ([GDS blog](https://gds.blog.gov.uk/2026/06/04/data-maturity-the-foundation-for-ai-ready-public-sector-data/)). No public prototype has been named. It covers legislation and case law, not Approved Documents.

## What builders_lab should use, borrow or ignore

The table gives a verdict for each option. The verdicts are judgments based on the evidence above, not tested results.

| Option | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| legislation.gov.uk API directly | **Use** | Free, OGL, has dated versions and outstanding-change data. Call it from your own server at a polite rate with a proper User-Agent. |
| legislation-mcp-ts (TNA) | **Borrow** | OGL, so its code and its guides on past versions and outstanding effects can be reused with credit. Copy its tool design for legislation lookups. |
| cyanheads | **Borrow** | Apache-2.0. Good model for handling "nearest earlier version", editorial status and citations like "SI 2019 No. 419". |
| Pipeworx legislation | **Borrow one idea** | Return the true "valid from" date on every answer. |
| paulieb89 uk-legal-mcp and govuk-mcp | **Borrow ideas, do not depend** | MIT. Useful pattern for finding Approved Document pages through the GOV.UK APIs. A third-party hosted server adds trust and uptime risk. |
| i.AI Lex | **Watch, do not depend** | Experimental, no SLA, runs models on its side, and ranked an old local Act first in one test. Its weekly Parquet files might help a one-off check. |
| Ansvar, flaviohtavares, openlaw, gb-eli, the toys | **Ignore** | Acts only, titles only, stale, or unproven. |
| LeoRickayzen uk-building-code-mcp | **Ignore** | Hobby project on an out-of-date PDF, no licence. |
| CoCounsel, Harvey, Legora, Lexis | **Ignore** | Per-seat, costly, US-first, and TR's terms ban the use you would make. |
| NBS, BSI | **Ignore for now** | No API, no price, BSI terms ban retrieval use. Cite standards by number and title only. Revisit only if users ask for standards text. |

Telling builders to add a public legislation MCP server next to the Builders connector would also cut across your own rules. Your regs-question skill answers only from your own library and does no web search. A second server would give the model a second, unchecked source with no edition dates and no `verify_answer` check. It would also add a third-party server to your security picture (ADR 0007).

## Conclusion

The market has split in a way that helps builders_lab. Fetching legislation is now a solved, free, commodity job: a dozen projects do it, and The National Archives has published its own OGL code. The hard part is the building layer, and nobody has done it. Nobody turns the Approved Document PDFs into dated, section-level text. Nobody tracks which edition applies to a job by its application or start date, links each paragraph to the regulation it supports, or covers HSE guidance such as L153 and HSG264. Nobody lists the standards each part cites, or speaks to a small builder in plain English or Romanian. That is the gap you must fill, and your OGL corpus is already the right tool for it. Your MCP server is the right channel.

Two practical points follow. First, put most of the effort into Approved Document versions and transitions, not legislation search. The 2026 Part L and F editions, which apply from March 2027, and the September 2026 Part B change mean the same question can have two right answers depending on the job's dates. Second, every legislation answer should carry the "up to date to" date and any outstanding changes from legislation.gov.uk. That is a cheap guard, and most third-party servers do not show it clearly. Watch the GDS and TNA Alpha. If an official, supported legislation MCP server appears, you could swap your own legislation fetch for it. It is unlikely to cover the Approved Documents.
